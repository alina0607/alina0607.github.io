Overview

Free-hand sketch recognition is image classification for drawings made by people, not cameras: a few quick strokes, no texture or colour, and everyone draws a cat differently. This CNN recognises sketches across all 345 categories of Google's Quick, Draw! dataset, trained from scratch in PyTorch. It is the vision half of Sell Anything.

Data at Scale

Quick, Draw! holds 50.4 million drawings (39.5 GB as 28×28 bitmaps). Each category is one file of about 100 MB, so the pipeline downloads only the slice it needs with HTTP range requests. The current model trains on 1,725,000 drawings (5,000 per class) and is evaluated on 172,500 held-out drawings (500 per class); a retrain on 4.83 million drawings is running.

1,912,985 parameters. Training: SGD with momentum and a one-cycle learning-rate schedule, with random rotation, shift and scale augmentation on the GPU.

Matching the Player's Strokes

A model is only as good as the match between what it trained on and what it sees. Player strokes are rasterised the way Quick, Draw! made its bitmaps: scaled, resampled, simplified with Ramer–Douglas–Peucker, centred with padding and anti-aliased down to 28×28. Rendering the dataset's raw strokes this way reproduces the official bitmaps with a pixel correlation of 0.996.

In Progress — Why CNN Beats MLP, Measured

A step-by-step notebook rebuilds the model from a random guess upward and measures what each step buys. Baselines so far (3 categories): random guessing 34.3%, nearest average image 86.3%. Next, a controlled comparison of an MLP and a CNN:

  • Matched parameters, optimiser and epochs: rule out "the CNN is just bigger" or "trained longer"
  • Pixel shuffle: scramble every image with one fixed permutation; an MLP should not care, a CNN should collapse, showing the CNN's advantage is spatial structure
  • Shift test: move drawings a few pixels and compare the drop
  • Data efficiency: 100 vs 800 drawings per class
